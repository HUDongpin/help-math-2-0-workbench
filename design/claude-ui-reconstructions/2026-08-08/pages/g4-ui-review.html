This is not an accident. The component says so directly: “The modern toolbar is an additional large-target surface, not a reason to turn the visibly preserved source controls into dead artwork.” Three real problems motivated that decision, and all three deserve solving — the original hit areas are unlabelled bitmap hotspots, they shrink when the stage scales down, and they are too small for touch.

But the fix chosen was addition rather than substitution. The duplicate surface appears whenever the viewport is at least 1280 px wide — precisely the case where the stage is at native size, the hit areas are full size, and none of the three problems apply. The companion is present exactly where it is least needed, and it is present alongside the original rather than instead of it.

Why it matters here specifically

HELP Math’s learners are the ones least able to absorb the overhead

HELP Math is an intervention product. Its audience is English language learners and students working below grade level — the population for whom every additional English label is a comprehension cost and every additional choice is a decision competing with the mathematics. A general-audience app can absorb a cluttered chrome. This one cannot.

Two well-established effects apply. Hick’s Law says decision time grows with the number of options, so a screen offering forty-four choices is measurably slower to act on than one offering nine. The redundancy effect in cognitive load theory is sharper still: presenting the same information twice does not merely fail to help, it degrades learning, because the learner spends effort reconciling the two presentations. A child who sees two buttons labelled “Map” has to work out whether they do the same thing. That effort is subtracted from negative numbers.

The screen also leaks its own construction. A nine-year-old is currently shown “Flash transport parity: not established”, “Frame inspection is a modern audit aid: it pauses, silences, and reconstructs the renderer”, and “Package status: Controlled JavaScript MVP; this is not a strict-fidelity or public-release claim.” These are migration-QA notices sitting in the learner’s field of view.

The proposal

Split the audience, then let the artwork be the interface

One structural change resolves most of the list. The lesson currently serves two audiences from one screen — a fourth grader learning negative numbers, and a migration reviewer checking JavaScript output against Flash frame by frame. Give them separate modes.

Learner mode — the default

  • The original 800×600 shell, unchanged, as the only control surface at native size.
  • A slim bar above it: lesson title as real text, section name, one progress strip, EN/ES, exit.
  • Two large buttons below it: Back and Next. Nothing else.
  • Completion becomes implicit — finishing a page marks it, and “Mark reviewed” disappears.
  • Section tabs and the page dropdown fold into the Course Map, behind the Map button already in the artwork.
  • When the stage scales below full size or the pointer is coarse, the artwork goes inert and one labelled bar replaces it.
  • The narration button gets a designed home in the top bar instead of floating over the lesson.

Reviewer mode — opt-in

  • Everything that exists today, plus room to grow.
  • Frame inspector: −20 / +20, the scrub slider, the frame counter.
  • The transport-parity notice and package-status banner, where they are actually useful.
  • Restart lesson, release-gate state, strict-complete member counts.
  • Reached by query flag or role. Never by a student.

Same stage, same artwork, same fidelity claim — 24 fewer controls. The map, key terms and calculator do not disappear; they stay exactly where HELP Math 1.0 put them, inside the artwork. Everything removed from the screen is either a duplicate of something still on it or a reviewer tool.

Getting there

Ordered by ratio of effect to effort

1

Make the modern toolbar a fallback instead of a companion

Drop wideFunctionalCompanion from the presentation rule, so the labelled toolbar appears only when the stage has actually scaled down or the pointer is coarse — the two cases it was built for. In the same change, mark the legacy hit layer inert whenever the modern surface takes over, so exactly one surface is live at a time. This single edit removes 14 controls on desktop and clears the 12 target-size failures on phone and tablet, because the undersized hotspots stop being controls at all.

Cost: the e2e suite asserts the toolbar at wide viewports in roughly a dozen places, and g4-l3-whole-lesson.test.ts:563 pins the exact source text of the presentation call. Both need updating alongside the policy — those tests are guarding the current decision, so changing the decision means changing them deliberately, not working around them.

2

Gate the reviewer tooling behind an explicit mode

candidateMode already flows into the shell but only swaps a sentence of prose. Promote it to a real audience switch. Behind it: the frame inspector and its slider, the transport-parity badge and paragraph, the package-status footer, and Restart entire lesson. In front of it: nothing that mentions frames, renderers, releases or fidelity. This removes seven controls and every line of engineering language currently facing students.

3

Collapse five navigation systems into two

A learner currently has section tabs, a 39-item dropdown, the course map, the stage’s own prev/next hands, and a second prev/next pair below. Keep linear by default, map on demand: one Back, one Next, and the Course Map that already lives in the artwork. Delete the page dropdown — a list reading “12. VB08 · Negative Numbers” is an authoring tool. Move the section tabs inside the map, where the same eight sections are already listed with progress ticks.

While there: IR, RW, VB, IN, TI, GS, TS and FQ are internal taxonomy. Students should read “Learn It”, not “IN”.

4

Give the narration button a designed home

For an English language learner, the narration is the lesson. Right now it appears only when Chrome blocks autoplay, as an unstyled 160×40 px button dropped over the top-right of the artwork — the most important control on the page, and the only one nobody designed. Give audio a permanent, styled place in the top bar that reflects its state, so it reads the same whether autoplay succeeded or not.

5

Make progress implicit and drop “Mark reviewed”

Three buttons sit side by side, two of which overlap: “Mark reviewed” and “Reviewed & next”. Nine-year-olds do not carry a mental model of reviewing a page. Mark a page complete when its animation finishes, show that in the single progress strip, and let Next just mean next. One fewer control, one fewer concept, and the primary action stops competing with a secondary one for the same visual weight.

6

Raise the floor on type, targets and contrast

For a K–5 audience set 16 px as the absolute minimum type size, and 18 px for anything a student reads to make a decision; the 11.2 px section tabs and 10.4 px dropdown label are well under it. Size every primary learner control at 48×48 px rather than the 24×24 WCAG floor — small hands, trackpads and shared classroom hardware all argue for headroom. Fix the “← Previous” label, which fails AA at 3.66:1, and give the wrapping “VOLUME” label room so it stops breaking mid-word.

7

Give the lesson a real title

“Counting on Numbers” is painted into the chrome PNG. It cannot be selected, translated into Spanish, resized, or read aloud by a screen reader. On a bilingual product whose premise is making math language visible, the lesson’s own name is the one string invisible to the language layer. Render it as text in the top bar and leave the artwork as artwork.
